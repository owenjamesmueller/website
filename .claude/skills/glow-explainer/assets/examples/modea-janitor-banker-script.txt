In 2014, a janitor in a small Vermont town died, and left his library and his hospital six million dollars. Nobody had any idea he had money. A few years earlier, a Harvard-educated Wall Street executive, one of the best-paid men in finance, went bankrupt. So how does a man who mopped floors end up richer than a man who ran Wall Street? The executive did everything you're supposed to do. Top school. Top job. Top salary. And every time his salary went up, so did his life. He borrowed heavily, and bought a mansion with eleven bathrooms and two elevators. Then the crash hit. The money stopped. The bills didn't. The janitor did something so boring, nobody noticed. He drove a used Toyota. He held his coat together with safety pins. And for decades, he quietly bought small pieces of big companies, and never sold them. Every piece paid him a little. Those payments bought more pieces. One man looked rich, and was broke. The other looked broke, and was rich the whole time. Because wealth isn't what you see. It's what you don't.
